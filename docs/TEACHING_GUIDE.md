# Crossroads: The Silk Roads — teaching guide

Crossroads follows a fictional merchant household around AD 200. Its central question is: **How can goods travel far when people depend on local knowledge and relationships?** Samira's household offers a human scale for examining transport, trade, migration, and evidence.

Suggested audience: students aged roughly 13 and above, or younger students with a reading partner. This is an instructional judgment, not a measured reading-level claim. The glossary supports terms such as broker, guarantor, credit, oasis, and relay trade. Story mode is suitable for discussion; Challenge mode adds pressure from provisions, travel time, debt, and carrying capacity.

## What students should practice

- Explain why money, skills, and trusted introductions can solve different problems.
- Trace how settled workers and traveling partners connect a household across cities.
- Compare routes using water, pasture, season, distance, and local information.
- Distinguish an object's long journey from the shorter journeys of its carriers.
- Identify what a dated historical source can support and what remains a reconstruction.

## A flexible classroom session

1. **Orient.** Locate Merv, Bactra, the Pamir approaches, Kashgar, and Dunhuang on the map. Ask students to predict where animals might need additional food or water. Explain that the north and south branches follow oasis networks around the Tarim Basin.
2. **Play in pairs.** One student makes a choice; the other states the expected benefit, cost, and person affected. Switch roles each encounter. Students should sometimes disagree: there is no single moral answer to every caravan dilemma.
3. **Pause after each chapter.** Each chapter's application question asks students to transfer a concept to a new example. Discuss why the explanation fits, including why the alternatives are incomplete. A wrong first answer receives an explanation and does not prevent the journey from continuing.
4. **Compare routes.** If possible, assign some pairs the northern branch and others the southern branch. The north adds Kucha and Turfan encounters about records, pooled travel, and water. The south adds Yarkand and Khotan encounters about ownership, services, supplies, and assessing jade. Both end at the Dunhuang region after omitted travel stages.
5. **Debrief with evidence.** Ask students to choose an entry from their journey, identify its source link, and separate the documented setting from the invented transaction. Compare one choice with a reasonable alternative using the resources and people involved.

The game includes 26 base encounters. A full eastbound journey now includes two additional encounters on the selected northern or southern route, for 28 decisions. Several earlier endings deliberately let a household settle or hand goods onward; reaching Dunhuang is not the only worthwhile outcome.

## Using the learning feedback

“Concepts demonstrated” records the number of correct first answers to five application questions. It is a small formative check, not a mastery, intelligence, or history-exam grade. Unanswered questions do not count as demonstrated concepts. Review the explanations and the student's reasoning instead of comparing the number alone.

For a discussion or written exit ticket, ask:

> A merchant has money but no trusted contact in the next oasis. A second merchant has little money but a reliable resident partner. Construct a situation in which either one would be better placed to continue. Identify what evidence you would need before deciding.

Look for three things: a concrete benefit and cost, an explanation connecting local conditions to the choice, and a clear limit on what the student knows. These criteria allow different defensible answers.

## Historical framing and limits

**Documented setting:** Bactria under Kushan rule; Han Dunhuang and its frontier connections; oasis routes around the Tarim Basin; movement of goods, people, and religious traditions; and the importance of transport and local relationships.

**Fictional reconstruction:** Samira, the other named characters, dialogue, individual illnesses, exact bargains, wage rates, prices, toll amounts, and consequences. A source link supports the historical topic; it does not document the encounter itself. The game does not establish how common every depicted household arrangement was.

**Later comparisons:** Some detailed surviving commercial letters, accounts, and legal evidence postdate AD 200. For example, the Sogdian Ancient Letters discussed in the cited scholarship date to AD 313. UNESCO dates documents in Dunhuang's library cave from AD 405 to 1002. Such sources illuminate later networks and suggest questions about earlier practice; they cannot establish that every detail was already present in Samira's period.

**Geographic compression:** Map points are approximate locations of cities or regions. Lines connect selected places and omit many intermediate settlements, river crossings, and alternative tracks. Wakhan marks a valley region, not one compulsory route. The Turfan-region marker is near Yar/Jiaohe. The southern route omits numerous oasis stages east of Khotan. Modern coordinates orient the reader without claiming ancient borders or precise street locations.

**Time and quantities:** Journey days represent approximate game time. Travel transitions compress uneventful weeks; individual encounters are selected vignettes, not a verified travel diary. Inventory units and scores are game measures rather than historical weights, exchange rates, or physiological measurements.

**Health episode:** The high-altitude scene uses current medical evidence to explain the danger of confusion and breathlessness. It now offers ways to descend; it does not present waiting at altitude as a reliable response to severe symptoms. This is explicitly modern explanatory context, not evidence for ancient diagnosis or treatment. The child's fever scene does not identify a disease or claim that an unspecified remedy cures it.

## Source guide

The game uses institutional heritage descriptions and specialist scholarship as readable starting points. They are secondary explanations of historical evidence. An ancient letter, coin, manuscript, inscription, or excavated object is a different kind of source. Ask who made it, when, why, where it was found, and which claim it supports.

| Source | Used for | Limit to discuss |
| --- | --- | --- |
| [UNESCO: About the Silk Roads](https://www.unesco.org/en/silk-roads/about-silk-roads?hub=196704) | Broad network setting, exchange, and cultural transmission | A multi-period overview does not prove the game's particular transactions. |
| [Iranica: Bactria before Islam](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/bactria/bactria-i-pre-islamic-period/) | Bactria, Kushan context, and regional connections | Regional history does not identify Samira's exact road or household. |
| [Iranica: Commerce in the Parthian and Sasanian periods](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/commerce-iii/) | Ancient trade goods, tolls, and commercial relationships | The article emphasizes gaps in Parthian evidence; detailed later legal evidence should not be silently moved backward in time. |
| [Iranica: Sogdian Trade](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/sogdian-trade/) | Changing merchant networks, Kushan intermediaries, and AD 313 letters | Merchant roles changed over centuries; “Silk Roads” does not mean one unchanging system. |
| [UNESCO: Chang'an–Tianshan Corridor](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1442/) | Northern route geography, water systems, and surviving sites | Inscribed sites span different periods. The game does not place every surviving structure in AD 200. |
| [Iranica: Jade](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/jade-i/) | Khotan as a jade source and variation in the material | Several written descriptions are later than the setting; the game's inspection and prices are invented. |
| [UNESCO: Dunhuang](https://en.unesco.org/silkroad/content/dunhuang) | Han commandery, gateway role, and dates of later documents | Library-cave evidence is later than this campaign. |
| [International Dunhuang Programme: Manuscripts](https://idp.bl.uk/manuscripts/) | Examples of the kinds of surviving documents researchers study | A collection contains many dates and contexts, not one account of AD 200. |
| [CDC Yellow Book: High-Altitude Travel and Altitude Illness](https://www.cdc.gov/yellow-book/hcp/environmental-hazards-risks/high-altitude-travel-and-altitude-illness.html) | Modern explanation of serious altitude symptoms and descent | This is health guidance, not a source about ancient medical knowledge. |

Map orientation uses the following references: [UNESCO Ancient Merv](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/886/), [UNESCO Balkh/Bactra](https://whc.unesco.org/en/tentativelists/1928/), [UNESCO corridor site coordinates](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1442/maps/), [Iranica Kashgar](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/kashgar-kasgar-town-in-xinjiang/), and [Getty TGN Dunhuang](https://www.getty.edu/vow/TGNFullDisplay?english=Y&subjectid=7001969). Wakhan, Yarkand, and Khotan are representative regional or oasis-center markers. They should not be used for route measurement or navigation.

## Questions beyond the game's scope

Who grew the food and tended the water channels? Whose labor is hidden when an object appears in a distant market? How might gender, wealth, legal position, and language change someone's ability to travel? What would an archaeological object reveal that a merchant's letter might omit? These questions extend the household story without treating it as a complete account of every Silk Roads society.
